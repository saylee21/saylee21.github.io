It’s just a loop

What Inkling 2 Mini changed, explained from scratch.

What is looping?

Think of an AI model as a long assembly line, where each worker does one small step on your sentence. To make a model smarter, labs usually hire more workers, which means a bigger and more expensive machine.

A looped model does something sneakier: it sends the work through the same few workers again instead of hiring new ones. Same size, more thinking.

↳ Instead of building a taller building, ride the same elevator more than once.

The model behind all this is Inkling 2 Mini, released on November 20, 2026 by Thinking Machines with open weights, so anyone can download it.

It has 26.4B parameters in total, but only 3.4B are active per token (5.4B once you count the repeated passes), and it runs its middle layers three times for every token. Here are the results:1

  • It beats every open-weights model under 100B parameters on DeepSWE v1.1 (48.2%, a coding-agent benchmark) and the Vals Index v2.2 (46.5%).
  • It ties Qwen3.8-27B on the Artificial Analysis index (20.1) with about 5x fewer active parameters.2
  • The whole model is about 20 GB, so it fits on a 32 GB laptop.
  • Stanford researchers measure “intelligence per watt”: useful answers per unit of energy. Inkling scored 0.703 on an NVIDIA DGX Spark, ahead of Qwen3.6-35B-A3B at 0.430.1 One caveat: Stanford got early access, Inkling ran at MXFP8 precision with low reasoning effort, and the other models ran at different precisions. A strong result, but not a perfectly even race.
Where does this sit? It’s an architecture choice (how the model is wired). It is decided during training but paid back during inference, when the finished model answers you. Who should care, in my reading: inference and serving engineers, local-AI and agent builders, and researchers studying loops.

How was it done before?

A normal model is a tall stack of layers. Text is chopped into small pieces called tokens, and each token travels down the stack once. Every layer has its own weights (numbers learned during training), so a 48-layer model stores 48 different sets.

To get smarter, you had three main options. Go bigger: more layers and weights, which means a bigger GPU. Think out loud: write a long chain of thought (CoT, step-by-step reasoning in words) before answering, which costs tokens, time and money. Or use a mixture of experts (MoE: many specialist sub-networks, only a few switched on per token), which is cheaper to run but still has to be stored.

Same story every time: more thinking meant more storage or more words.

What did Inkling 2 Mini do differently?

It reuses layers. The trick is called weight tying: one set of weights used in several places. Send the token through a few middle layers again and you get more depth without storing more. It’s like re-reading a hard sentence. You don’t need a new brain, just another pass.

The real model has 24 layers in four blocks of six. The first and last blocks run once; the two middle blocks run three times, so 24 stored layers give 48 layer visits per token.

Each pass gets a “message” (the previous pass’s output) through a learned gate (a valve) that starts closed, so training begins like a normal model and opens up slowly.

The team also pruned two-thirds of the experts (384 down to 128) and trained mostly in 4-bit precision (quantization: storing numbers with fewer bits), which is how it fits in about 20 GB.1 3

What running three times produces

First, more serial depth: how many computation steps the model can do in a row before it must write a word. In the lab’s variable-loop research version, the longest task it could solve with no written reasoning at all went from 16 seconds of human work at one pass to 37 seconds at three.

Second, better learning per unit of compute: at training step 2,000, a non-looped model needed 1.51 to 1.91x as much compute to match the loss (the training error score) of the middle-loop version. Third, shorter written thinking: on GPQA Diamond science questions it answered correctly, reasoning dropped from 3,703 tokens at one pass to 1,731 at three, with no loss in legibility.

Beyond three passes, most tasks gained little, though a step-by-step lookup task kept improving up to ten.1

Is this pretraining or post-training? What about backprop and loss?

Looping is baked into training (the stage that sets the weights), not added afterwards. The lab lists it as one of two changes to its normal recipe, along with pruning.1

The research variant trained with one to five passes in midtraining and did reinforcement learning (RL, learning from reward) at three passes; after pruning came supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and 2.2T tokens of RL. The post doesn’t say at which exact step looping switches on.

For backpropagation (sending the error backward to adjust the weights), the gradient has to flow back through every pass, a bit like backpropagation through time in recurrent networks. Because the weights are tied, each one collects adjustments from all three passes (general weight-tying math, not from the release). That can make training unstable, which is what the closed-at-first gate is for.

As for the loss function (the score training tries to lower), the post doesn’t describe a special one. Ouro, by contrast, adds an entropy-regularized objective (a penalty that keeps depth choices from collapsing) to learn how many loops to use.6

What it means for inference

Generating text is autoregressive (one token at a time) and usually memory-bandwidth bound (limited by how fast weights move from memory, not by arithmetic).

Looping helps and hurts here. It helps because the file is small (about 20 GB, mostly in NVFP4, a 4-bit format) and because shorter reasoning means fewer tokens to generate (3,703 to 1,731 in one test).

It hurts because repeated layers are read again: the lab’s own speed ceiling (614 GB/s of memory bandwidth, batch size one, BF16 weights) already includes repeated reads across loops.1

The KV cache question (the model’s short-term memory of the text so far) has an answer now. Each pass keeps its own KV cache, so the 24-layer model carries 48 KV caches and 48 conv-state caches (the short convolution’s running state), and vLLM serves each pass’s cache like any other layer’s.

The trade-off is capacity: on the research checkpoint, KV room drops from ~352K tokens at one pass to ~175K at three. A shared-cache option on the research checkpoint recovers some of that (~194K), but the shipping Inkling 2 Mini is fixed at three passes and rejects the toggle.5

On balance, the lab found the non-looped model needed 140% more inference compute (2.4x) to reach the same loss on long agentic traces. That’s a compute comparison at equal quality, from their own unpruned test, not a measured token saving or a speedup.1

What the serving stack had to change

The loop did not need a new cache manager. vLLM registers each extra pass of a core layer as its own attention layer with its own KV cache, and the KV cache manager and prefix caching treat those entries like any other. That is the quiet reason day-0 support was possible: from the cache manager’s side, the model looks like a 48-layer model, not a special case.

The kernels are where the work went. A naive implementation launches four message reads and four controllers, several kernels each, for every core layer on every later pass. vLLM reads all four messages in one attention-residual kernel, stacks the controllers’ weights so their projections run as batched GEMMs, and fuses the gated merge with the RMSNorm of the sublayer it feeds. It reuses Inkling 2 Small’s sublayer kernels and works at every tensor-parallel size, including a single GPU. Above one GPU, small batches also fuse the all-reduce over NVLink multicast.

On the shipping model the loop is not a dial. Both research-checkpoint options are rejected there: loop_num_passes and loop_share_cache exist only on TML-d24-varloop-research and are set through --hf-overrides. The native DSpark speculator also requires the default three passes, because its draft reads the target’s hidden states at four fixed layer visits.

What is new here for an inference engineer

Depth is now a property of the weights, not a runtime knob. On the shipping model you cannot trade passes for latency: every request does the same 48 layer visits, so a batch never diverges on loop count, and capacity planning has one number to budget instead of a range.

The serving win, if there is one, comes from the output side. On correctly answered GPQA Diamond questions the lab measured 3,703 reasoning tokens at one pass and 1,731 at three, with legibility unchanged. Decode is memory-bandwidth bound and runs one token at a time, so roughly half the output tokens is roughly half the decode steps on that workload. The loop costs 2.1x per decode step at batch 32 (3.81 to 8.11 ms), which is what the shorter output is paying for.

The combination that matters for agents is per-pass caches plus ordinary prefix caching: a long agent trace resends the same prefix every turn, and vLLM reuses it across the 48 cache layers without special handling.

What this does not prove: there is no independent throughput or cost-per-token number yet. The 614 GB/s figure is the lab’s theoretical ceiling at BF16 and batch one, not a measurement on your hardware, the timing table is one GB200 at 4K context, and the token saving was measured on one benchmark family, not on your traffic.

Did someone try this before?

Yes. Looping is not new, which is honestly the interesting part.

  • Universal Transformer (2018). The seminal paper: reuse one layer repeatedly. Thinking Machines cites it as the basis of its design.4
  • ALBERT (2019). Shared weights across layers to shrink a language model. It saved storage, not compute.8
  • Huginn (Geiping et al., 2025). A recurrent-depth model whose authors showed extra inference-time thinking kept helping, up to a compute load equal to a 50-billion-parameter model (as summarised in a later paper).7
  • Ouro (ByteDance Seed and others, 2025). Looped models of 1.4B and 2.6B parameters trained on 7.7T tokens, matching standard 4B and 8B models. Its authors noted that whether looping works at practical scale was still unproven.6

How did they end?

Each showed gains at small sizes, then stayed research projects. None reached a 26B-class model with open weights and broad tooling. That is what is new now.

What does it mean for the future?

  • A third way to scale. Models have grown with parameters and data; Ouro’s authors argue loop depth is a third axis.6
  • Strong local AI. A model of about 20 GB that handles agentic coding offline changes what a laptop can do.1
  • A new safety question. More silent thinking means less written reasoning to watch (monitorability: how well humans can oversee a model by reading its thoughts). At this size the lab found no meaningful drop, and released the models partly so others can check.1
  • What to watch. Independent benchmarks, serving costs once memory reads and caches are counted, and whether other labs follow. Chatter that some big closed models already loop is speculation, not fact.

Is looping just rumination?

Partly, and it’s a useful way to see it. Both mean going back over the same material. But human rumination (repeating a thought with no new information) tends to get stuck. A looped pass is trained to improve the answer: it receives the previous pass’s message through the learned gate, and training pushes the loss down when extra passes help.1 It’s healthy when each pass adds something. The overthinking risk is real too: most tasks gained little beyond three passes. That’s why researchers want models that choose how many loops to use. Ouro, for example, learns when to stop.6

Would five passes be even better, and why three?

Usually only a little. In the lab’s variable-loop research model, the longest task solved with no written reasoning rose from 16 to 37 seconds of human work between one and three passes, then stayed roughly flat. Each extra pass still costs compute and another read of the looped weights, so the gain per pass shrinks while the price doesn’t. Three is also what the model was mostly trained for: the research variant saw three passes most often, saw 1, 2, 4 and 5 less, and did its reinforcement learning at three, so the lab warns other pass counts may be understated.1 The lab doesn’t explain the choice beyond this evidence, so read three as a sweet spot, not a law. Ouro used four.6 And it depends on the job: the step-by-step lookup task kept improving up to ten loops.

↳ The takeaway: a normal model gets smarter by getting taller. A looped model gets smarter by going around again.
